Hyperspace 3D Glasses Plan

We want viewers at the projection cart to see the scene in 3D through simple colored glasses. This page sums up the research plan: how it works, which colors to try, what to buy, and the first test to run.

How it works

One picture on the screen, a different picture in each eye

The computer draws the scene twice, once for each eye. It tints each copy a different color and merges them into one image. Colored lenses let each eye see only its own copy. The brain fuses the two slightly different views into depth.

  1. 1Two camerasDraw the scene from two spots, about an eye-width apart.
  2. 2Tint each viewLeft view keeps only red. Right view keeps only green and blue (cyan).
  3. 3MergeBoth tinted views are added into one picture.
  4. 4ProjectorThe cart's projector throws the merged picture.
  5. 5ScreenWithout glasses it looks like a doubled, color-fringed image.
  6. 6GlassesRed lens on the left eye, cyan lens on the right.
  7. 7Brain adds depthEach eye gets its own view. The brain fuses them into one 3D scene.
Ghosting

A faint copy of the other eye's picture leaking through a lens. It is the main thing we are trying to keep low.

What each lens does

Each lens passes its own picture and blocks the other

These four views are made by actually filtering the same merged picture, the way a lens does. The red lens keeps only red light. The cyan lens keeps only green and blue light.

Skip pale pink

Pale pink lenses look lovely and bright. They are bright because they let lots of green through, and green is the other eye's picture. That shows up as ghosting. In the green part of the light, LEE 035 Light Pink lets through 48.6%, 036 Medium Pink 27.4% and 002 Rose Pink 10.1%. A deep magenta like LEE 795 lets through only 0.49%. Chart below.

Software

Start with the simplest color split, then upgrade the recipe

The way the two tinted views get mixed is a "recipe". We build it so recipes can be swapped as a setting, without changing the rest of the program.

First

Channel split

+=

The left view keeps only its red. The right view keeps only its green and blue. Add them. Very simple, and good enough for a first test.

Next

Better published recipes

+

Recipes published by researcher Dubois blend a little of every color from each view. They keep more color and cut ghosting. Ready-made versions exist for red/cyan, green/magenta and amber/blue.

Later

Our own custom recipe

+ measured projector light =

Once we measure our lenses and our projector, the same method computes a recipe made for exactly our setup.

Where Hyperspace is today

The active app, hyperspace-python, draws the scene from one camera. For 3D it has to draw two views every frame, one per eye, then mix them. There is no 3D or color-mixing code yet, so everything above is proposed. The engineering agent decides how to build it.

Troubleshooting

If it looks wrong, try this first

What you seeLikely fix
Faint double image (ghosting)
Turn off the projector's BrilliantColor or white boost. Use a cleaner lens: LEE 172 or 183 instead of 115, which leaks about 1% red. Try two layers of gel. Reduce the amount of depth. Later, a custom recipe.
One eye looks darker
Brighten the dim eye's picture in software, or add a pale neutral layer over the brighter lens. Example: LEE 106 passes 9.3% of light, LEE 172 passes 25.4%.
Looks flat, no depth
Space the two cameras further apart, within comfort limits. Check the viewer too: about 5–10% of people cannot see stereo depth.
Depth is inside out
Glasses on backwards? Red goes on the left eye. If not, swap left and right in the settings.
Colors look wrong
Switch to the better recipe. Check the software undoes the screen's brightness curve before mixing, and redoes it after. The cyan gels pass only about a quarter of green, so the right eye can look blue-heavy.
Fine on a monitor, wrong on the projector
Expected: their light differs. Tune on the cart's own projector and keep a separate setting for each display.
Each viewer sees it differently
Seats and eye spacing vary. Keep depth modest for the closest seats. Offer clip-ons for people who wear glasses, and a no-glasses option.
Safety

Treat these as viewing filters, not eyewear

  • Theater gels are lighting filters. They have no eyewear, impact or UV rating.
  • Make no sun, UV, impact or "eye protection" claims. Label the glasses "Not sunglasses. Do not wear outdoors."
  • Keep glass away from eyes. Use plastic lenses for wearing. The Edmund filters are glass, so they are for measuring only.
  • Clean shared glasses between viewers and throw away scratched pairs. Paper frames and gels cannot be wiped with alcohol.
  • Take the glasses off before walking around in the dark. They cut light and change colors.
  • Keep gels away from the projector's hot air. Cut gel edges can be sharp.
  • Seat viewers, keep sessions short, and offer a no-glasses option for anyone who feels uncomfortable.
Still unknown

What we don't know yet

  • How our projector behaves. Nothing has been measured, and the kind of projector changes ghosting a lot.
  • Whether the color-pair ranking holds on a projector. The comparison study used TV and computer screens.
  • What store-bought lenses actually filter. No seller publishes it, so we test the glasses we buy.
  • The real screen size and seat distances at the cart, which set how much depth is comfortable.
  • Real shipping costs and delivery dates to San Francisco.
  • How much brighter one eye is than the other. Studies say magenta/green balances best but give no numbers.
  • How color-blind viewers get on. No study was found.
  • Whether gel dyes are safe to wear close to the eyes for long. No maker says.
This week

The first experiment you can run this week

The first check needs no new code. It shows how cleanly each lens blocks the other color on our own projector.

  1. Order today: Rainbow Symphony red/cyan paper glasses, 10-pack ($7.50), and ProView plastic, 2-pack ($18.00). Expect them in about 2–4 business days (estimate).
  2. Lock the projector: pick one fixed color mode. Turn off BrilliantColor or white boost, dynamic contrast, eco mode and auto keystone. Write the projector model and settings down.
  3. Show one still image: left half pure red, right half pure cyan, filling the screen.
  4. Hold one lens over the line in the middle. The more completely the other half turns black, the less ghosting. Do it for each lens and each pair of glasses. Take a phone photo with exposure locked, for the record.
  5. Write it down in the experiment log template (experiment_log.csv). Once two-view drawing exists, run the depth check next: a square that should float in front of a frame.
