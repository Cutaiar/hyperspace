## Q1. Calibrated filters: Kodak Wratten 2 (Edmund), Thorlabs colored glass, Edmund dichroic and cast-plastic filters. Availability, prices, transmission data

### Takeaway
Edmund Optics still sells Kodak Wratten 2 filters as 75 x 75 mm, 0.10 mm sheets, in stock. The #25 red and #47 blue cost $131 each; the #44A cyan costs $266. Kodak publishes a downloadable spectral-density curve (graph PDF, not tabular data) for each number, including 25, 44A, 47, 58 and 32. The best-value calibrated wearable options are Edmund's 50 mm 0° dichroic filters (Red #30-634 at $44.50, Cyan #39-912 at $44.75) and Edmund's 2" x 2" red/green/blue CR-39 cast-plastic filters ($22.75). Both come with published specs. The dichroics are 2 mm BOROFLOAT glass, so they are not impact-rated, and their passbands shift with viewing angle. Thorlabs colored glass is a reference-grade option, not a wearable one: Ø25 mm or 2" squares, 2 mm Schott glass.

### Inferences
- For a prototype pair, cutting a 75 x 75 mm Wratten 2 sheet gives roughly two eyepieces per sheet at ~35 x 35 mm or larger. Red #25 ($131) plus cyan #44A ($266) is about $400 for one to two pairs. The cost is justified only as a "golden reference" pair whose spectra are documented by Kodak curves. The filters are delicate (0.10 mm, scratches, humidity clouding), so they are a poor fit for shared or public use.
- Edmund dichroic red #30-634 plus cyan #39-912 (~$89 per pair, 50 x 50 mm) have the sharpest, best-specified passbands, with crosstalk under 1% inside the spec bands. They are glass on 2 mm BOROFLOAT, which brings weight, breakage risk and a mirror-like reflective look. Interference filters' edges shift toward blue at off-normal angles. That is general thin-film physics, not stated on these pages, so verify it with the vendor's AOI data before relying on wide-angle viewing.
- CR-39 cast plastic is the same resin family used for ordinary eyeglass lenses. But Edmund offers only red, green and blue, so it can make red/green or red/blue pairs, not red/cyan. Edmund publishes "Transmission >20%" and a curve image only. The ~$45.50 pair (2 x $22.75 for a red and a green or blue square) is the cheapest documented-spectrum wearable option found.
- Thorlabs RG610 (sharp red longpass) could pair with a BG-series or dichroic cyan as a lab-reference eye pair for spectrometer calibration. The parts are too small and heavy for wearables, apart from tiny Ø25 mm "pinhole" viewers.

### Inferences
- A single "% light transmission" figure does not show whether a red sheet leaks green or blue, which is the anaglyph crosstalk that matters. TAP Dark Red (49.9%) and Dark Blue (48.1%) could be usable, but TAP's "Light Blue" (85.5%) probably passes some red, so it would ghost. Measure with a spectrometer, or at least a phone camera through each filter against projector R, G and B patches, before committing.
- 3 mm acrylic is thick and heavy for eyewear and is not impact-tested. Laser-cut acrylic eyepieces in a cardboard or 3D-printed frame are fine for a supervised seated demo, but not for an unsupervised consumer product.
- Plexiglas 2423 red has a ~600 nm cut-on. That is close to Wratten #25 territory and favorable for a red eye. Its 0.060" (1.5 mm) sheet is a good thickness for lens inserts.
- For a matching cyan eye there is no well-documented low-cost acrylic. A dichroic cyan (#39-912) or the Wratten #44A remains the documented choice. The alternative is to measure several TAP blues and greens.

## Q3. Dichroic film, laser/notch filters, and wavelength-multiplex (Infitec/Dolby/Omega) as a deferred alternative

### Takeaway
3M's dichroic film line (DF "Chill"/"Blaze") is decorative architectural film with color-shifting appearance. It is not a spec'd anaglyph filter, and a DFA-12 product number could not be confirmed. Wavelength-multiplex 3D (Infitec, Dolby 3D, Omega/Panavision) uses multi-band interference filters on both projector and glasses. It is related to anaglyph (both split by wavelength), but it uses narrow interleaved R, G and B bands per eye to keep full color. Glasses are not readily retail-purchasable. Infitec's site returned a 404 today, and Dolby's price history is from 2008-2011. Recommendation: DEFER.

### Cited Findings
- 3M dichroic film: technical data sheets describe the DF "Blaze" (warm) and "Chill" (cool) films with or without adhesive (Rev. G, December 2019). A US product page lists a DF CHILL code. No source confirmed "DFA-12" or its discontinuation. — [3M Dichroic DF tech data sheet](https://multimedia.3m.com/mws/media/861295O/3m-dichroic-df-tech-data-sheet.pdf); [3M US product page](https://3m.com/3M/en_US/p/d/v000171747)
- Infitec: wavelength-multiplex technology "is based on the fact that the visible light spectrum can be divided into different wavelength ranges using steep-edged optical filters". It needs filters in the glasses and in the projector, "except laser projectors". — [stereoscopy.com Infitec FAQ](https://stereoscopy.com/faq/interference-filters.html)
- Omega Optical developed a separate color band-pass system used by Panavision for 3D cinema. Its bands differ from Infitec's. — [Projector Reviews passive 3D part 9](https://www.projectorreviews.com/home-theater-and-projectors-the-technical-side/passive-3d-projection-part-9/)
- Dolby 3D projects left and right images with slightly different R, G and B wavelength sets. The glasses filter for each eye's set. Historical exhibitor pricing: reusable glasses at a US$27.50 list (reduced from $39.00), later $17; next-generation glasses at ~$12 or lower with a bundle. All from 2008-2011 trade press. — [Widescreen Review 2008](https://widescreenreview.com/news_detail.php?id=17169); [TV Technology](https://www.tvtechnology.com/be-blogs/8511); [NBC News 2011](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna43583102)
- infitec.net/en/ returned HTTP 404 ("This Page Does Not Exist") when fetched 2026-10-17. — [infitec.net](https://infitec.net/en/)

### Inferences
- Relation to anaglyph: anaglyph gives each eye a broad complementary band (e.g., red vs. green+blue), so color is lost. Wavelength multiplex gives each eye its own narrow R, G and B triplet, so each eye sees full color. That needs either a 6-primary projector (two filtered lamps or an RGB laser projector with two laser sets) or a filter wheel. A single standard 3-primary projector cannot do it, so the approach belongs to a later phase.
- An RGB laser projector's narrow primaries make classic anaglyph with dichroic glasses work unusually well. If the red, green and blue laser lines are known, an Edmund-style dichroic red/cyan pair with edges placed between the green and red lines could give very low crosstalk. That is an inference; verify it against the projector's measured spectrum.

### Gaps
- No current (2026) retail source or price for Infitec, Dolby 3D or Omega 3D glasses was found. Infitec's current corporate status is unclear (site 404).
- No verified laser-line or notch-filter eyewear products for RGB laser projectors were captured. Edmund and Thorlabs sell notch filters, but none were priced in this pass.

## Q5. Safety and regulatory: FDA 21 CFR 801.410, ANSI Z80.3, Z87.1, ISO 12312-1, hygiene, comfort

### Takeaway
21 CFR 801.410 requires impact-resistant lenses for eyeglasses and sunglasses: a 5/8-inch, ~0.56 oz steel ball dropped from 50 inches must not fracture the lens. FDA's 2010 guidance says FDA "in general, does not require impact testing of novelty eyewear" if it is not intended to give corrective power or protection from bright sunlight, and if dark-lensed items carry a "not for use as sunglasses" warning. FDA still "strongly recommends" impact testing. CPSC rules (16 CFR 1500/1501) may also apply. Anaglyph viewers for a projection installation fit this novelty category, provided no sun or UV protection claims are made. Making any "blocks UV" claim pulls the product into sunglass regulation. ANSI Z80.3 covers nonprescription sunglasses and fashion eyewear (light attenuation). ANSI Z87.1 applies only to occupational safety eyewear. ISO 12312-1 covers sunglasses for solar-radiation protection. None was found to specifically address 3D glasses.

### Cited Findings
- Regulation text: "(2) In the impact test, a 5⁄8-inch steel ball weighing approximately 0.56 ounce is dropped from a height of 50 inches upon the horizontal upper surface of the lens. The ball shall strike within a 5⁄8-inch diameter circle located at the geometric center of the lens ... To pass the test, the lens must not fracture". Also: records of distribution of impact-resistant lenses must be kept 3 years, and test results kept 3 years. — [eCFR 21 CFR 801.410](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-H/part-801/subpart-H/section-801.410)
- FDA guidance "Impact-Resistant Lenses: Questions and Answers", issued September 2, 2010, nonbinding. Q12: "Is novelty eyewear subject to the impact testing regulations?" A: "FDA strongly recommends that you conduct impact testing of any glass or plastic lenses used in novelty eyewear ... In general, FDA does not require impact testing of novelty eyewear if: (1) the item is a novelty item, which is not intended to provide children (or other users) with corrective power or protection from bright sunlight; and (2) a warning label stating that the item is not to be worn outside as sunglasses to protect the eyes against strong sunlight is placed on eyewear that has dark-colored lenses. However, all novelty eyewear may be subject to regulation by the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) under ... (FHSA) ... 16 CFR 1500 ... and 16 CFR 1501" (small-parts rules for under-3s). — [FDA guidance PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/71020/download) (text extracted and read)
- Same guidance, Q14: children's toy sunglasses "intended to provide protection from bright sunlight, such as sunglasses sold or given away in fast food restaurants as promotional items, ... must meet the impact test". Q18: goggles claiming "Blocks UV" or "Blocks Sunrays" are regulated as sunglasses, "including impact resistance". Q11: clip-on sunglass lenses are subject to impact testing, with enforcement discretion when they can only be worn over impact-resistant lenses, outside, and no larger than those lenses. Q15: "Impact-resistant lenses are used in (1) eyeglasses, (2) sunglasses, and (3) noncorrective fashion eyewear", while safety lenses (industrial) have "more stringent" requirements. Q19: demonstration lenses "are typically not rendered impact resistant". — [FDA guidance PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/71020/download)
- FDA's suggested drop-ball certificate language (per a regulatory consultant) includes "Impact-resistant lenses are not unbreakable or shatterproof". Each import shipment of glasses or lenses should carry a drop-ball test certificate. — [Registrar Corp: FDA drop ball test](https://www.registrarcorp.com/blog/medical-devices/medical-device-registration/fda-drop-ball-test)
- ANSI Z80.3 scope (per the ANSI blog and webstore): nonprescription sunglasses and fashion eyewear with essentially plano lenses, "for light attenuation", plus frame and lens flammability and durability. It excludes products under Z87.1 and Z80.1. A 2025 edition (ANSI Z80.3-2025) is referenced, and the 2018 edition is listed as historical. Which edition is current was not fully confirmed. — [ANSI blog: Z80.3 sunglasses requirements](https://blog.ansi.org/ansi/ansi-z80-3-sunglasses-requirements/); [ANSI webstore Z80 listing](https://webstore.ansi.org/sdo/vc(ascz80))
- ISO 12312-1:2022 applies to "all afocal (plano power) sunglasses and clip-ons for general use" for solar-radiation protection. It excludes eyewear for protection against artificial light sources, sport eye protectors (ISO 18527), solar viewers (ISO 12312-2) and occupational eye protection (ISO 16321). Amendment ISO 12312-1:2022/Amd 1:2026 has been published (snippet). — [EVS: ISO 12312-1:2022](https://www.evs.ee/en/iso-12312-1-2022); [SCC standards DB](https://scc-ccn.ca/standardsdb/standards/8177934)
- ANSI Z87.1-marked clear safety glasses (e.g., UVEX S300CS) are fixed-lens products. A frame alone is not rated eye protection (snippet). — [Ferguson/frameless safety listing](https://www.ferguson.com/product/armateck-frameless-foam-lined-safety-glasses%2C-clear-lens-arm3854clr/9206232.html)
- Hygiene in cinema practice: XpanD said exhibitors could clean its glasses "in a washing machine or with antibacterial wipes". PCO Group collects used cinema glasses, discards badly scratched ones, and the rest "are cleaned, disinfected, repackaged and sent back". A Kooptech washer/dryer is described as recommended by Dolby and RealD. APO says its theme-park frames survive commercial dishwashers. All are vendor claims, not an independent hygiene standard. — [TV Technology: XpanD wipes](https://www.tvtechnology.com/news/xpand-offers-3d-glasses-with-handy-wipes); [PCO Group 3D glasses](https://www.pco-group.com/our-products/3d-glasses.html); [3dglassesonline plastic glasses](https://www.3dglassesonline.com/products/plastic-glasses/)
- Material chemical tolerance: Edmund says its CR-39 cast-plastic filters resist "Acetone, Acids, Alkali and Alcohols". Edmund says Wratten gels are easily scratched or contaminated and should be handled with gloves. — [Edmund cast plastic family](https://www.edmundoptics.com/f/optical-cast-plastic-color-filters/12257/); [Edmund Wratten family](https://www.edmundoptics.com/f/kodak-wratten-filters/11429/)
- Anaglyph comfort: Mark Simonson notes anaglyphs "don't work very well if they have strong colors in them—especially red". Reel 3-D's convention is "Red lens over Left eye". — [Mark Simonson: How to make 3D anaglyphs](https://www.marksimonson.com/notebook/view/howtomake3danaglyphs/); [Reel 3-D](https://www.stereoscopy.com/reel3d/anaglyph-glasses.html)

### Inferences
- **Prototype-safe practices.** Use these for a supervised installation or private demo:
  - Make no UV, sun, "eye protection" or "impact-resistant" claims.
  - Label the glasses "Not sunglasses. Do not wear outdoors or for sun protection." The darker red/cyan pairs are arguably "dark-colored lenses" under FDA Q12.
  - Prefer plastic (CR-39, acrylic, polyester or gel) over glass dichroics in front of the eyes. If glass dichroics are used, mount them in a hand-held lorgnette or a viewer held at arm's length rather than worn eyewear, or put a clear impact-resistant polycarbonate shield on the eye side. This is a design suggestion and has not been tested.
  - Keep small parts away from under-3s (CPSC 16 CFR 1501).
  - Advise users to remove the glasses before walking around in dark spaces, since the filters cut overall light and distort color perception.
  - Seat viewers and limit session length. Offer a no-glasses fallback for people who feel discomfort. Include a general photosensitivity notice if the content flashes. Specific photosensitive-epilepsy guidance was not researched here.
- **Hygiene.** Choose materials that tolerate wipe-down: CR-39 is alcohol-resistant per Edmund. Wratten gels and paper frames do not tolerate it. Acrylic is generally sensitive to solvents and can craze; no source was captured for that here, so test on an offcut. For shared glasses, the cinema model is wash or wipe between users and discard scratched pairs.
- **Consumer product (later).** If glasses are sold or given away at scale, do the following. These are inferences from the guidance, not legal advice; confirm with FDA (CDRH) or a regulatory consultant.
  - Keep them clearly "novelty", and still drop-ball test the lenses per FDA's recommendation.
  - Keep the 3-year distribution records only if they are positioned as eyewear subject to 801.410.
  - Avoid any sunglass claims.
  - Consider ANSI Z80.3 frame flammability and durability as best practice.

### Gaps
- No FDA statement, warning letter or case specifically about 3D or anaglyph glasses was found. The novelty-eyewear guidance is the closest authority.
- The full text of ANSI Z80.3-2025 and ISO 12312-1 (paywalled) was not reviewed. Whether either mentions 3D or filter viewers is unknown.
- No authoritative source on alcohol compatibility of acrylic or polycarbonate eyewear lenses was captured. Photosensitivity and visual-comfort guidance specific to anaglyph was not researched in depth.
